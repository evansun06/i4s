## Final recommendation

**Panel recommendation:** If you go ahead, adopt only an adult, under-40-lb, foster-tested dog with a calm, adaptable temperament—not a puppy. Given your three office days, one-bedroom apartment, daycare option, and possible move within a year, adoption can work, but only if you treat the dog as a fixed life constraint rather than something to “fit in.”

**Why:** The apartment size is less important than unattended time, transition stress, daycare suitability, and future housing uncertainty. Daycare at $45/day can solve the office-day coverage problem, but it becomes a regular cost—about $135/week—plus you still need backup care. The possible move is not disqualifying, but you must be willing to choose future housing, neighborhood, and routines around keeping the dog.

**Strongest objections:**  
- **Daycare may not work.** Some dogs find it stressful or may fail intake, so don’t choose a dog hoping daycare will fix alone-time issues.  
- **A move could disrupt everything.** If there is any realistic chance you’d give up the dog because of housing, jobs, cost, or city logistics, don’t adopt now—foster or dog-sit instead.  
- **A puppy is a bad fit.** Your schedule makes puppy needs too demanding unless you have extensive time and support.

**Concrete next steps:**  
1. Set a full monthly dog budget, including daycare, vet care, food, gear, pet deposits, and moving costs.  
2. Call the daycare about requirements, waitlists, trial days, and rejection policies.  
3. Identify backup care before adopting.  
4. Look for an adult fostered dog described as house-trained, low/moderate energy, apartment/elevator tolerant, dog-neutral/social, able to settle, and not separation-anxious or high-drive.  
5. Prefer foster-to-adopt.  
6. First month: quiet routine, gradual alone-time practice, daycare trials, then only move to the real office schedule if the dog is stable.
